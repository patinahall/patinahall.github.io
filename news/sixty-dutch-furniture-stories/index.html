Furniture Library

Sixty Dutch furniture stories, from Rietveld's packing boards to a chair printed in one line

The Furniture Library has gained forty-five objects and fifteen of the people who drew them: a century of Dutch furniture, from a chair made of crate boards to a seat printed in one thick line of reclaimed plastic.

Published 30 October 2026

A century of arguing about how a chair should be made

Few countries have packed as many furniture ideas into as little space as the Netherlands, and the Furniture Library has just gained sixty stories about them — forty-five objects and fifteen of the people who drew them. Each one was researched, written, illustrated and reviewed from the sources up, and each one was published in English, Dutch and German on the same day.

It opens where Dutch furniture keeps opening: with Gerrit Rietveld, who could not stop asking what the cheapest honest answer looked like. The Crate Chair was built from ordinary packing boards and sold on a completely different bargain. The Zig-Zag Chair is four wooden planes, and the entire difficulty is hidden at its corners. The Schröder Table turns coloured planes into a small room you walk around rather than something you look at. And the Steltman chair began as a mirrored pair for a jeweller, which is why it only fully makes sense standing beside its opposite.

The factory had ideas of its own

The second thread is less famous and, we would argue, even more Dutch: the drawing office and the production line as places where design actually happens. Gispen 412 is a tubular outline that carries upholstery, leaves the space open and gives a little as you sit. Look beneath the familiar Result school chair and you find shaped sheet steel, and the collaboration of Friso Kramer and Wim Rietveld. Pastoe turned factory knowledge into storage the customer could compose, beginning at the corner. Auping's Cleopatra made a single bed restrained enough to belong in a room that is used all day.

Piet Zwart's Bruynzeel kitchen belongs here too: standard components fitted to domestic work with real rigour — and an efficiency that carried its own assumptions about who was doing that work. The page says so. A library that only flatters its subjects is not a library.

Then the material started answering back

The third thread is the one that made Dutch design famous all over again. Maarten Baas burned furniture and kept what survived the fire. Piet Hein Eek made the leftovers the whole point, with the work visible in the selection and the arrangement rather than in a finish. Jurgen Bey's Tree Trunk Bench notices that in most places the seat is already there, and only the backs need carrying in.

And then the machine became a co-author. Dirk Vander Kooij's Endless is a chair drawn in one thick line of reclaimed plastic; his Meltingpot presses yesterday's colour experiments into a table whose pattern belongs to the material rather than to a drawing. Christien Meindertsma found flax another job. Bertjan Pot's Random Light is the shape that stays behind after the ball it was built around has gone.

And the people, at proper length

Fifteen of the sixty are profiles rather than objects, because some of this cannot be told through a single chair. Friso Kramer, who treated the ordinary working day as a serious design subject. Hella Jongerius, who studies how colours and materials behave in each other's company. Gijs Bakker, whose jewellery and furniture read as one continuous argument about material and the body. Kho Liang Ie, whose chairs were always thinking about the whole room. Renny Ramakers, who showed that a good question can start a collection. Piet Hein Eek, who designed the place that makes the furniture.

Sixty more reasons to look twice at something you have walked past for years. That is what the Library is for.
