Personal profile

Niels van Kessel

Niels learned chess from his grandfather and discovered early that a move matters partly because of the route it leaves open. Historical films later drew him to fencing, where distance, timing and commitment turn abstract strategy into movement. Old maps bring both interests together on paper.

A way of thinking

A good route keeps its history visible.

Chess rewards the player who understands how one move changes the whole position. Fencing makes the same lesson immediate: distance closes, an opening appears and the path behind a decision cannot be separated from what happens next.

Chess & fencing

Strategy that has to survive movement

The chessboard first came to Niels through his grandfather. What stayed with him was not only competition, but the calm pleasure of tracing consequences: which square becomes vulnerable, which piece gains freedom and which attractive move quietly creates a problem three turns later.

Fencing began with a love of historical films and became a physical counterpoint to the board. A plan still matters, but it must adapt to rhythm, reach and another person's response. Niels likes the precision of a discipline that cannot hide indefinitely inside theory.

The vintage eye

Maps that remember the journey

Niels collects old maps because they show knowledge as a route rather than a finished answer. Borders move, names change, blank areas reveal uncertainty and the paper itself carries the evidence of folding, handling and travel.

He is especially fond of the visual language of the early twentieth century: disciplined lettering, measured colour and diagrams that try to make a complicated world navigable. The most interesting maps are useful and historical at the same time.

At PatinaHall

Helping every object keep its source

As Backend & Platform Engineer, Niels follows the route a product takes from an independent store through catalogue preparation to a public page. Titles, photographs, prices and availability may move, but the original seller and the reason for every transformation should remain clear.

His strategic patience helps with the irregular reality of vintage catalogues: one-off stock, incomplete descriptions and products that disappear when they sell. PatinaHall can do work quietly in the background, but it should never invent a shortcut that loses where an object came from.
