Personal profile

Anouk Verbrugge

Anouk has loved horses since childhood and still trusts the quiet conversation that happens before a rider asks anything of an animal. She says a single look can often reveal a horse's mood. When she wants a slower kind of observation, she paints in watercolour and lets colour gather in transparent layers.

A way of noticing

Attention begins before the first mark is made.

A horse cannot be understood by following a diagram, and a watercolour cannot be hurried into clarity. Both ask Anouk to notice small changes in posture, pressure, light and tone before deciding what the next gesture should be.

Horses & watercolour

Learning to read a mood at a glance

Anouk's affection for horses began in childhood. Riding taught her that confidence can be quiet: a shift in weight, the direction of an ear or one steady look may say more than a dramatic movement. She values the moment of understanding that arrives before words.

Watercolour offers a different version of that conversation. Pigment moves, paper keeps a trace of every decision and the light often comes from what has been left untouched. Anouk prefers building an image patiently to covering every uncertainty with another layer.

The vintage eye

The lives held inside small things

Victorian jewellery fascinates Anouk at the scale of a clasp, a worn edge or a tiny private symbol. The beauty is inseparable from the fact that somebody chose it, wore it and kept it. An object becomes more interesting when its history remains close enough to imagine without pretending to know every detail.

Old letters hold a similar charge. Handwriting, paper and a fold line turn language into a physical thing. This is why she gravitates toward antiques that have not been polished into anonymity: their character lives in the relationship between appearance and use.

At PatinaHall

Keeping an object's character on screen

As Frontend Engineer, Anouk works where photographs, measurements, condition notes and seller context become something a person can actually see and use. She shapes the rhythm of a gallery, the clarity of a next step and the way the same story survives both a wide desk and a narrow phone.

Her eye for mood and material keeps PatinaHall from flattening one-off furniture into interchangeable cards. A thoughtful crop, honest scale cue and comfortable line of type should help a reader look more closely at the object, not admire the interface instead.
